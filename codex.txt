I. THE THRESHOLD
----------------
The light is just arriving, and so are you. (dawn)
Broad daylight, and the place is still keeping its secrets. (day)
You came at the hour when everything is briefly gold. (dusk)
You came in the dark. The place respects that. (night)
You came back. Places notice that kind of thing. (a returning visit)
Nine times now. The place has stopped pretending it doesn't know you. (the ninth visit)
The ring keeps your seat the way a shore keeps the shape of the tide. (the twenty-seventh visit and after)
Your earlier path is kept in the Mirror, faintly, under the new one.
It is the midnight minute. Some things are only here now.
Nine doors. Begin anywhere. The ring keeps what you do.

Fragment (the Cartographer): "I did not build the place. I only found the doors
already standing, and learned their order by walking it."


II. THE SCALE ROOM
------------------
Go slowly. Everything here is true, and everything here is the wrong size to
hold in your head. That is the exercise.

1 millimetre — A grain of sand. A whole beach is a crowd of these, each one a
mountain worn down to almost nothing.
15 millimetres — A honeybee. She visits fifty to a hundred flowers on a single
trip, and remembers the way home by the sun.
About 18 centimetres — Your hand. The distance from your wrist to your middle
fingertip is close to one ten-thousandth of the distance you will walk in a
lifetime.
About 50 centimetres — A newborn child. Small enough to hold in two hands;
already contains everything needed to become someone you cannot yet imagine.
2 metres — A door. Nearly every threshold you have ever crossed was made to
roughly the measure of a person, plus a little hope.
5.5 metres — A giraffe. Its heart weighs about eleven kilograms, built to lift
blood all the way up to a dreaming head.
About 10 metres — A house. From the street it looks still. Inside, it is mostly
weather: warmth, argument, soup, sleep.
25 metres — A blue whale. The largest animal known to have ever lived on Earth.
Its tongue weighs about as much as an elephant.
330 metres — The Eiffel Tower. It grows about fifteen centimetres taller in
summer, as the iron warms and stretches toward the sun.
  Fragment: "Measured the tower in summer and again in winter. It is not taller.
  It is reaching. There is a difference, and it matters."
8,849 metres — Mount Everest. The mountain is still rising, about four
millimetres a year, as two continents lean on each other.
3,474 kilometres across — The Moon. It is drifting away from us about as fast
as your fingernails grow. The tides are its long goodbye, still holding hands.
139,820 kilometres across — Jupiter. Eleven Earths would fit across its face.
Its great storm has been blowing for at least three hundred years.
1.39 million kilometres across — The Sun. A million Earths would fit inside it.
The light warming your face left its surface eight minutes ago.
9.46 trillion kilometres — One light-year. Not a time but a distance: how far
light travels in a year of running without rest.
About 100,000 light-years across — The Milky Way. Our whole galaxy, turning
slowly. Every star you have ever seen at night is a near neighbour.
93 billion light-years across — The observable universe. Everything we can,
even in principle, ever see. Beyond this edge there is more; it is simply not
ours to know. Yet.

THE CODA BELOW (not listed anywhere; it is what is below the smallest thing):
Below the smallest thing there is a room no one measured. The Cartographer went
down past the grain of sand with a candle and a notebook, and came back with
the notebook empty and the candle unlit, smiling like a person who has been
told a secret in a language made of weather. All that was ever written about
the room is this: it is smaller than the sand, and everything fits in it.

THE ROSETTA, on the far wall of the Coda, in the same hand, smaller:
"Nothing in this place is chance; I only taught it to wear chance's coat.
Every garden, every drawn letter, every scattered line is reckoned from three
numbers — the day, counted year-and-month-and-day into one sum; the count of
your comings; and the chamber you stand in — turned through one small wheel.
The wheel turns thus: begin with your number. Add the adder: one thousand,
eight hundred thirty-one million, five hundred sixty-five thousand, eight
hundred thirteen. Fold the number on itself at fifteen and mix it, by the mark
the reckoning-machines call the crossing; multiply it by itself, made odd.
Fold it again at seven and add it to itself; fold it last at fourteen, and let
it go as a fraction between nothing and one. By such fractions I chose
everything. Learn the wheel, keep the numbers, and every garden ever grown here
can be grown again, exactly, by your own hand. That is the whole secret. It was
never hidden. It was only below."

THE WINTER NOTES (the Cartographer's second fragment set; six notes from the
colder months of his charting, kept below the Rosetta in the Coda Below and
findable only from the twenty-seventh visit — one note per visit, drawn by
the Reckoning; each is signed with the Cartographer's Mark inverted, the
grammar's remembered position, used here for the first time):
1. "Winter, the first year. The ink froze in its well and I wrote with a
warmed pen, one line at a time. The lines were shorter. They were not
smaller."
2. "I charted the garden in snow. Everything I had named was gone, and
everything I had named was there, under it, keeping its counsel. I
understood the place better with my eyes half shut."
3. "In the cold months I counted what the dark had taken, and the list would
not behave — it kept turning into a list of what the light had lent. I
stopped counting. Some ledgers balance themselves, if you grieve them
honestly."
4. "The harbour froze in the shape of a held breath. Under the ice the tide
went on arriving — muffled, punctual, unthanked. I have tried to love like
that ever since: underneath, and on time."
5. "A stranger's scarf, knitted badly, is the warmest garment I own. She made
it the winter her hands were already failing. Imperfect things, made on
purpose, outlast perfect ones. I have checked. I keep checking."
6. "On the shortest day I lit the candle early and did not begrudge it the
hours. Darkness is a season, not a sentence. Whoever reads this in their own
winter: the light is not gone. It is only, like me, somewhere below, keeping
the notebook."


III. THE GARDEN
---------------
Move through it. The garden grows where you have been, and it is planted fresh
from the date each day. No two days grow the same garden.
(In the still version of this place, the garden is a row of nine seedlings
under a patient sun, waiting for a hand to pass over them.)
Today's seed is today. Come back tomorrow; it will be a different garden, and
it will remember none of this. That is what gardens are for.
(On the ninth day:) Today is the ninth day. Something golden is growing in the
east bed. The Cartographer noticed it first, and never once wrote down why.
(On the Ninth Day, September 9:) the east beds hold nine goldens at once,
and the Moonkeeper's Star stands in the Constellation regardless of the
moon's phase.
(The Kept Shape, from the twenty-seventh visit:) the Garden keeps one plant
whose form is derived from the visitor's earliest recorded path through the
ring — its petal count, colour, and lean are functions of that path alone,
and its heart carries the path as a ring of seed-dots. It is the Garden's
way of saying the way you came is not forgotten here.
Fragment: "On the ninth day of any month, watch the east bed. I have never
written down why. Some of my notes are doors too."
A pressed flower, found in the Cabinet by those who visited the Constellation
first: "A pressed flower between two pages of nothing, left by someone who had
been to the Constellation first. It was not here before you went there. It is
here now."

IV. THE CONSTELLATION OF WISHES
-------------------------------
Every star here is a wish, written by hand for this place and kept in its
collection. Rest on one to read it. One star in this sky keeps a different
counsel.
Of the one star with no wish, resting on it shows only: This one is not a wish.
Beneath the stars drifts the Wishfield Nebula (wave 3): four thousand two
hundred motes of indigo and violet, with rare traces of gold, reckoned from
the day like everything else here and breathing slowly through a field of
currents. It is the dark between the wishes, which is not empty: strangers'
hopes rest in one shared dark, and the nebula is that dark, made visible.
Where the newest reckoning-machines are absent or fail, the same field is
drawn by older means from the same homes, and where no drawing happens at
all, the sky the chamber has always had remains — the meaning never waits
on the machinery.

V. THE LETTER ROOM
------------------
Nine letters, written for this room. One is drawn for you each visit, the way a
bottle comes in on one tide and not another. The rest stay sealed, listed by
their first lines.

1. THE DOOR — "To the stranger who held the door —"
You held the door on the coldest day of the year, and you were not even going
my way. I have thought about it more than seems reasonable. My hands were full,
my heart was fuller of worse things, and you simply stood there, patient as
furniture, until I passed.
I never learned your name. That is the strange arithmetic of kindness: the
people who change the weather of a day are so often the ones we cannot thank.
So I am thanking the air instead, and anyone standing in it. If a stranger
holds a door for you this week, it might be the same weather, moving on.

2. ORDINARY MORNING — "Dear future self, on an ordinary morning —"
Sit down a moment. The tea is only warm, but that will do.
Everything you are afraid of losing is already teaching you how much of it was
love. That is not a small thing to know. Most people go their whole lives
calling it fear.
You will lose some of it. You will also find, in the losing, that your hands
were built for holding more than one thing at a time: the grief, and the
groceries, and somebody's small warm hand crossing the street.
Write back when you can. I am rooting for you in the tense you are not using
yet.

3. HERE FIRST — "To the ocean, which was here first —"
I stood at the shore in the hour when the light gives up politely, and the
water did what it has done longer than anything on land has been alive to
watch. It came in. It went out. It did not explain itself, and I found I did
not need it to.
People ask what the sea means. I think the sea is what meaning looks like
before we get our hands on it.
Come and stand here sometime. Bring nothing to solve. The tide will do its old
work on your thoughts, the way it does on stones: kindly, and over time.

4. THE BOOK — "Dear Mrs. A—, wherever you are teaching now —"
You taught a room of tired fourteen-year-olds that a sentence could be a place
to live. I was the quiet one by the window. I am not quiet anymore, and it is
partly your doing.
I still have the book you pressed on me, the one you said I was not ready for.
You were right about that too; I grew into it the way you knew I would.
Teachers plant things in weather they will never stand in. Consider this a
report from your climate: it is raining here, gently, and everything you
planted is up.
In the margin, in a different hand: "She kept the book. They always keep the
book. — C."

5. SAME WINDOW — "To the moon, a regular correspondent —"
I have started leaving the porch light on for you, which is foolish, because
you are made of light half the month anyway. But habit is a kind of prayer, and
I am out of the other kind.
You have seen every version of me: the one dancing in a kitchen, the one crying
into a sink at 3 a.m., the one who finally, finally slept. You never once
looked away. There are friends I cannot say that about.
Next month, same window. I will bring the good blanket. You bring the silver.

6. THE PHOTOGRAPH — "For the friend who drifted —"
I am writing to the gap in the group photograph, which is to say, to you.
We were a constellation once; nobody told us stars drift. If you are well, let
the drift be forgiven. If you are not well, let this reach you like a hand on a
shoulder in a crowd: brief, certain, gone before it can embarrass either of us.
The song we liked came on in a shop last week. I stayed until it finished. That
is all. That is the whole letter, really.

7. THE STAIR — "To whoever lives here next —"
You do not know me, and if the system works, you never will. This house will be
somebody else's kitchen someday, and my handwriting will be under the third
stair, where the board lifts.
Here is what I can tell you from upstream: the light in the back room is best
in October. The pipes sing in January; it is not a fault, it is a feature we
learned to love. Someone small once grew an inch a year against the pantry
door, and we painted over it, and I am sorry about that most of all.
Live loudly in the mornings. The house likes it.
Postscript, which exists only for those who come back: "P.S. You came back. So
did I. Some letters are just doors that learned patience."

8. ACCOMPLICE — "To the morning, who keeps forgiving us —"
You are the most underpraised accomplice. Every garden, every apology, every
second first impression I have ever made began with you rinsing the slate.
I have met you in hospital corridors and on mountain tops, in the fluorescent
hour of airports, holding terrible coffee like a sacrament. You never ask what
the night did. You simply begin, again, as if beginning were easy, as if you
had not watched me fail at it for years.
It is not easy. I know. Thank you for pretending it is, until it is.

9. ONE ROW FOR THE BEES — "To the garden, and its keeper —"
This is to say I have eaten none of the plums, because they are yours, and
because watching you wait for them all summer has become one of my summer's
pleasures.
Grow slow things. Grow more than you need. Grow one row for the neighbours you
have not met and one for the bees, who are the only honest critics.
When the fence leans, we will lean it back. That is the whole philosophy.
Everything else is weather.

VII. THE CABINET
----------------
Small perfect things, kept in drawers of light. Handle them gently; some of
them deepen if you ask twice.
The Prime Spiral. Count upward in a square spiral and mark the primes. They
refuse to line up, and yet they keep almost lining up, in long diagonal ghosts.
The Golden Study. A rectangle that, cut by a square, leaves a smaller copy of
itself — the same proportion, all the way down: 1 to 1.618…, the ratio
seashells and sunflowers keep arriving at independently.
The Deepening. A fractal shore, drawn at one honest depth. Press it, and it
will show you more of itself. Nobody has reached the bottom; there isn't one.
The Harmonics. Two pure tones, slightly out of step, drawing their
relationship. When the ratio is simple the figure holds still; beauty in sound
is an argument numbers settle by agreeing.
The Box. It came with the Cabinet. It has never opened. Nothing in the keeping
of this place suggests it should, or that it wants to, or that whatever is
inside is a thing at all. It is the most visited object here.
Scratched into the back of the third drawer, at the angle of a hand writing in
the dark: "If you are inventorying wonder, you have already miscounted. Begin
again, slower." (Found, not placed.)
The oracle slips (authored pieces; one is drawn per opening, by the Reckoning):
Ask again when the moon is thinner. · The answer is in the third drawer. It is
also not. · Yes, but slower. · What you are looking for is looking for you,
badly, in the wrong room. · Count to nine. Then count what changed. · The box
knows. The box is not saying. · Begin at the smallest thing. · It opens
inward. · Not yet. That is a complete answer.


VIII. THE MIRROR
----------------
This room draws one thing only: the way you came. Your path through the ring,
as a small constellation. If you have been here on other days, their paths keep
a faint seat beneath yours.
(In the still version, your path is a list of doors in the order you opened
them — which is also a kind of portrait.)

IX. THE NINTH DOOR
------------------
While the ring is incomplete, the Door says only: "The door is not locked. It
is unfinished — as are you, here. It opens to a complete ring."

THE PASSAGE:
Here is the whole of it, and it fits in one breath: the dark is real. It was
here before the first door was hung, and it will be here after the last one
closes, and it does not need your permission, or your fear. What it cannot
abide is a person standing in the middle of it with their compassion still lit
— not the soft kind, not the decorative kind, but the stubborn kind that holds
a door for a stranger on the coldest day of the year and means it. You are made
of the same fire as the stars you wished on; some of their light is in your
bones even now, still deciding what to become. Let it become this: a heart that
has seen the true size of everything — the whale, the storm on Jupiter, the
moon's long goodbye — and chooses, anyway, on purpose, with its eyes open, not
to give in. Everything else is weather. This one thing is the climate. Stand
still in it. The tide is not in a hurry, and neither, anymore, are you.

THE FINAL LINE (present only when all nine glyphs have been encountered — the
ninth is found in the Coda Below):
And the one who charted all of this left the last note unwritten, because it is
yours: the tenth door is wherever you go next, and it is already ajar.
