Below the smallest thing there is a room no one measured. The Cartographer went down past the grain of sand with a candle and a notebook, and came back with the notebook empty and the candle unlit, smiling like a person who has been told a secret in a language made of weather. All that was ever written about the room is this: it is smaller than the sand, and everything fits in it.

On the far wall, in the same hand, smaller, as if for whoever came this far down and no one else, the Method: “Nothing in this place is chance; I only taught it to wear chance’s coat. Every garden, every drawn letter, every scattered line is reckoned from three numbers, the day, counted year-and-month-and-day into one sum; the count of your comings; and the chamber you stand in, turned through one small wheel. The wheel turns thus: begin with your number. Add the adder: one thousand, eight hundred thirty-one million, five hundred sixty-five thousand, eight hundred thirteen. Fold the number on itself at fifteen and mix it, by the mark the reckoning-machines call the crossing; multiply it by itself, made odd. Fold it again at seven and add it to itself; fold it last at fourteen, and let it go as a fraction between nothing and one. By such fractions I chose everything. Learn the wheel, keep the numbers, and every garden ever grown here can be grown again, exactly, by your own hand. That is the whole secret. It was never hidden. It was only below.”

“The sky over the threshold I did not reckon: it stands where your place and your hour put it, by numbers older than the wheel.”

Letters

To the stranger who held the door:

You held the door on the coldest day of the year, and you were not even going my way. I have thought about it more than seems reasonable. My hands were full, my heart was fuller of worse things, and you simply stood there, patient as furniture, until I passed.

I never learned your name. That is the strange arithmetic of kindness: the people who change the weather of a day are so often the ones we cannot thank. So I am thanking the air instead, and anyone standing in it. If a stranger holds a door for you this week, it might be the same weather, moving on.

Dear future self, on an ordinary morning:

Sit down a moment. The tea is only warm, but that will do.

Everything you are afraid of losing is already teaching you how much of it was love. That is not a small thing to know. Most people go their whole lives calling it fear.

You will lose some of it. You will also find, in the losing, that your hands were built for holding more than one thing at a time: the grief, and the groceries, and somebody’s small warm hand crossing the street.

Write back when you can. I am rooting for you in the tense you are not using yet.

To the ocean, which was here first:

I stood at the shore in the hour when the light gives up politely, and the water did what it has done longer than anything on land has been alive to watch. It came in. It went out. It did not explain itself, and I found I did not need it to.

People ask what the sea means. I think the sea is what meaning looks like before we get our hands on it.

Come and stand here sometime. Bring nothing to solve. The tide will do its old work on your thoughts, the way it does on stones: kindly, and over time.

Dear Mrs. A, wherever you are teaching now:

You taught a room of tired fourteen-year-olds that a sentence could be a place to live. I was the quiet one by the window. I am not quiet anymore, and it is partly your doing.

I still have the book you pressed on me, the one you said I was not ready for. You were right about that too; I grew into it the way you knew I would.

Teachers plant things in weather they will never stand in. Consider this a report from your climate: it is raining here, gently, and everything you planted is up.

In the margin, in a different hand: “She kept the book. They always keep the book. C.”

To the moon, a regular correspondent:

I have started leaving the porch light on for you, which is foolish, because you are made of light half the month anyway. But habit is a kind of prayer, and I am out of the other kind.

You have seen every version of me: the one dancing in a kitchen, the one crying into a sink at 3 a.m., the one who finally, finally slept. You never once looked away. There are friends I cannot say that about.

Next month, same window. I will bring the good blanket. You bring the silver.

For the friend who drifted:

I am writing to the gap in the group photograph, which is to say, to you.

We were a constellation once; nobody told us stars drift. If you are well, let the drift be forgiven. If you are not well, let this reach you like a hand on a shoulder in a crowd: brief, certain, gone before it can embarrass either of us.

The song we liked came on in a shop last week. I stayed until it finished. That is all. That is the whole letter, really.

To whoever lives here next:

You do not know me, and if the system works, you never will. This house will be somebody else’s kitchen someday, and my handwriting will be under the third stair, where the board lifts.

Here is what I can tell you from upstream: the light in the back room is best in October. The pipes sing in January; it is not a fault, it is a feature we learned to love. Someone small once grew an inch a year against the pantry door, and we painted over it, and I am sorry about that most of all.

Live loudly in the mornings. The house likes it.

P.S. You came back. So did I. Some letters are just doors that learned patience.

To the morning, who keeps forgiving us:

You are the most underpraised accomplice. Every garden, every apology, every second first impression I have ever made began with you rinsing the slate.

I have met you in hospital corridors and on mountain tops, in the fluorescent hour of airports, holding terrible coffee like a sacrament. You never ask what the night did. You simply begin, again, as if beginning were easy, as if you had not watched me fail at it for years.

It is not easy. I know. Thank you for pretending it is, until it is.

To the garden, and its keeper:

This is to say I have eaten none of the plums, because they are yours, and because watching you wait for them all summer has become one of my summer’s pleasures.

Grow slow things. Grow more than you need. Grow one row for the neighbours you have not met and one for the bees, who are the only honest critics.

When the fence leans, we will lean it back. That is the whole philosophy. Everything else is weather.

Tide

Tonight the moon is doing what it has always done.

The heart keeps the same hours as the tide: it fills, it empties, and it is pulled by something far away that loves it without speaking.

“I stopped counting the days I was here and started counting moons. The numbers got smaller and truer.”

Ninth

0 / 8

Here is the whole of it, and it fits in one breath: the dark is real. It was here before the first door was hung, and it will be here after the last one closes, and it does not need your permission, or your fear. What it cannot abide is a person standing in the middle of it with their compassion still lit, not the soft kind, not the decorative kind, but the stubborn kind that holds a door for a stranger on the coldest day of the year and means it. You are made of the same fire as the stars you wished on; some of their light is in your bones even now, still deciding what to become. Let it become this: a heart that has seen the true size of everything, the whale, the storm on Jupiter, the moon's long goodbye, and chooses, anyway, on purpose, with its eyes open, not to give in. Everything else is weather. This one thing is the climate. Stand still in it. The tide is not in a hurry, and neither, anymore, are you.

And the one who charted all of this left the last note unwritten, because it is yours: the tenth door is wherever you go next, and it is already ajar.
